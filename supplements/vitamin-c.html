Vitamin C (ascorbic acid)

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble vitamin found in fruits and vegetables such as citrus, peppers, kiwi and broccoli. Humans cannot make it and must get it from food.

Class
Water-soluble vitamin
Evidence summary
Deficiency (scurvy): established. Colds in the general population: no prevention, small effect on duration.
Review status
Agent draft, 2026-10-31. Scientific review required.

What it does in the body

Vitamin C is required to make collagen, carnitine and some neurotransmitters. It is an antioxidant and improves absorption of non-heme iron from plant foods.

Who may be at risk of deficiency

  • Diets with very few fruits and vegetables.
  • Smokers, who need more vitamin C.
  • People with severe malabsorption, alcohol use disorder or on dialysis.
  • Deficiency causes scurvy: fatigue, bleeding gums, poor wound healing and easy bruising.

Forms in supplements

Supplements contain ascorbic acid, mineral ascorbates or ester forms. Studies have not shown clear advantages of one form over another.

What human trials show

  • Systematic reviewA Cochrane review of 29 trials found that regular vitamin C did not reduce how often the general population catches colds; it shortened colds slightly (about 8% in adults).
  • Systematic reviewIn people under heavy physical stress, such as marathon runners, regular vitamin C halved the risk of colds in a small number of trials.
  • RandomizedStarting vitamin C after symptoms begin has not shown consistent benefit.

Observational evidence

  • Higher fruit and vegetable intake, and higher blood vitamin C, are associated with lower cardiovascular risk, but supplement trials have not confirmed prevention.

Safety, upper limits and interactions

  • Intakes above the upper limit can cause diarrhea and nausea.
  • High intakes may increase the risk of oxalate kidney stones in some people.
  • High intake may raise iron absorption, a concern in hemochromatosis.

Reference values (NIH, adults)

ReferenceValue
Men (RDA)90 mg/day
Women (RDA)75 mg/day
Smokers+35 mg/day over the RDA
Tolerable upper limit, adults2,000 mg/day

Regulatory context

  • United States: dietary supplements do not need FDA approval for safety or effectiveness before sale; manufacturers are responsible for safety and label claims.
  • Brazil: food supplements are regulated by Anvisa (RDC 243/2018 and its normative instructions), which define permitted ingredients, minimum and maximum amounts and allowed claims.

Limits of the evidence

Absorption falls as intake rises, so higher amounts do not raise blood levels proportionally. Many cold trials are small and use different regimens.

This profile is educational. It does not recommend doses, products or brands. Talk to a health professional before starting supplements, especially if you take medicines, are pregnant or have a chronic condition.
