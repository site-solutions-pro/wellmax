Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol)

Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is the form made in the skin after sun exposure and found in fish and fortified foods. The body converts it to 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the form measured in blood tests.

Class
Fat-soluble vitamin
Evidence summary
Deficiency correction: established. Extra intake when replete: large trials mostly negative.
Review status
Agent draft, 2026-10-31. Scientific review required.

What it does in the body

Vitamin D increases calcium and phosphate absorption in the gut and is needed for normal bone mineralization. Vitamin D receptors are present in many tissues, including immune and muscle cells, but most effects outside bone remain under study.

Who may be at risk of deficiency

  • People with little sun exposure or darker skin.
  • Older adults, whose skin produces less vitamin D.
  • People with fat-malabsorption conditions or after bariatric surgery.
  • People with obesity, in whom vitamin D is sequestered in fat tissue.
  • Severe deficiency causes rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults.

Forms in supplements

Supplements contain D3 (cholecalciferol) or D2 (ergocalciferol). Both raise blood levels; most studies suggest D3 raises and sustains 25(OH)D somewhat more.

What human trials show

  • RandomizedVITAL (25,871 US adults, median 5.3 years): vitamin D3 did not lower the incidence of invasive cancer or major cardiovascular events compared with placebo.
  • RandomizedA VITAL sub-study found no reduction in fractures among generally healthy adults not selected for deficiency or osteoporosis.
  • RandomizedCorrecting documented deficiency treats rickets and osteomalacia; this is the clearest benefit.

Observational evidence

  • Low 25(OH)D is associated with many diseases in observational studies, but randomized trials have not confirmed that supplementing replete people prevents them.

Safety, upper limits and interactions

  • Excess intake can cause high blood calcium, kidney stones and heart-rhythm problems.
  • Some medicines interact, including corticosteroids, orlistat and thiazide diuretics.
  • Blood-level targets are debated; the Endocrine Society 2024 guideline advises against routine testing in healthy adults.

Reference values (NIH, adults)

ReferenceValue
Adults 19–70 (RDA)600 IU (15 mcg)/day
Adults over 70 (RDA)800 IU (20 mcg)/day
Tolerable upper limit, adults4,000 IU (100 mcg)/day

Regulatory context

  • United States: dietary supplements do not need FDA approval for safety or effectiveness before sale; manufacturers are responsible for safety and label claims.
  • Brazil: food supplements are regulated by Anvisa (RDC 243/2018 and its normative instructions), which define permitted ingredients, minimum and maximum amounts and allowed claims.

Limits of the evidence

Most vitamin D trials enrolled people who were not deficient. Results do not answer whether people with very low levels benefit for outcomes beyond bone.

This profile is educational. It does not recommend doses, products or brands. Talk to a health professional before starting supplements, especially if you take medicines, are pregnant or have a chronic condition.
