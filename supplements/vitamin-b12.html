Vitamin B12 (cobalamin)

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is found naturally only in animal foods such as meat, fish, eggs and dairy, and in fortified foods. Absorption depends on stomach acid and a protein called intrinsic factor.

Class
Water-soluble vitamin (cobalamin)
Evidence summary
Deficiency correction: established, including oral and injected forms. Extra intake when replete: no shown benefit.
Review status
Agent draft, 2026-10-31. Scientific review required.

What it does in the body

B12 is needed to make red blood cells and DNA and to maintain the myelin that insulates nerves. It works with folate in the methionine cycle; deficiency raises homocysteine and methylmalonic acid.

Who may be at risk of deficiency

  • Older adults, who often have reduced stomach acid (atrophic gastritis).
  • Vegans and some vegetarians who do not eat fortified foods or supplements.
  • People with pernicious anemia, an autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor.
  • People after gastric or bariatric surgery, or with Crohn's disease affecting the ileum.
  • Long-term users of metformin or acid-reducing medicines.

Forms in supplements

Supplements contain cyanocobalamin, methylcobalamin, adenosylcobalamin or hydroxocobalamin. Oral high-amount B12 can correct deficiency even in many people with absorption problems; injections are also used.

What human trials show

  • RandomizedIn the Diabetes Prevention Program Outcomes Study, long-term metformin use was associated with B12 deficiency, supporting periodic monitoring.
  • RandomizedOral and intramuscular B12 both corrected blood levels in trials of people with deficiency.
  • RandomizedB-vitamin trials that lowered homocysteine did not reduce cardiovascular events in people without deficiency.

Observational evidence

  • Low B12 status is associated with anemia, neuropathy and cognitive changes; neurological damage can occur before anemia appears.

Safety, upper limits and interactions

  • No tolerable upper limit has been set because toxicity is not established.
  • High folic acid intake can mask the anemia of B12 deficiency while nerve damage progresses.
  • Metformin, proton-pump inhibitors, H2 blockers and colchicine can reduce absorption.

Reference values (NIH, adults)

ReferenceValue
Adults (RDA)2.4 mcg/day
Pregnancy (RDA)2.6 mcg/day
Tolerable upper limitNot established (N/A)

Regulatory context

  • United States: dietary supplements do not need FDA approval for safety or effectiveness before sale; manufacturers are responsible for safety and label claims.
  • Brazil: food supplements are regulated by Anvisa (RDC 243/2018 and its normative instructions), which define permitted ingredients, minimum and maximum amounts and allowed claims.

Limits of the evidence

Serum B12 alone can miss deficiency; methylmalonic acid or homocysteine are sometimes needed. Benefits in people without deficiency have not been shown.

This profile is educational. It does not recommend doses, products or brands. Talk to a health professional before starting supplements, especially if you take medicines, are pregnant or have a chronic condition.
