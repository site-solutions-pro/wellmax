Zinc

Zinc is an essential trace mineral found in meat, shellfish (especially oysters), legumes, seeds and whole grains. The body has no specialized zinc store, so regular intake is needed.

Class
Essential trace mineral
Evidence summary
Deficiency correction: established. Common cold: uncertain. Eye disease (AREDS formula): positive in a defined population.
Review status
Agent draft, 2026-10-31. Scientific review required.

What it does in the body

Zinc is required by hundreds of enzymes and is involved in immune function, protein and DNA synthesis, wound healing, cell division, and the senses of taste and smell.

Who may be at risk of deficiency

  • People with gastrointestinal disease or after gastrointestinal surgery.
  • Vegetarians, because phytates in grains and legumes reduce zinc absorption.
  • Pregnant and lactating women.
  • People with alcohol use disorder or sickle cell disease.
  • Signs of deficiency include growth delay, hair loss, diarrhea, skin lesions and impaired immunity.

Forms in supplements

Supplements contain zinc gluconate, sulfate, acetate, citrate or picolinate. The amount of elemental zinc differs between forms; labels list elemental zinc.

What human trials show

  • RandomizedAREDS: in people with intermediate age-related macular degeneration, a formula containing zinc and antioxidants slowed progression to advanced disease.
  • Systematic reviewA 2024 Cochrane review (34 trials) found no clear benefit for preventing colds and low-certainty evidence that zinc may shorten colds by about two days, with more side effects such as altered taste and nausea.
  • RandomizedZinc supplementation reduces diarrhea duration in children in low-income settings, where deficiency is common.

Observational evidence

  • Low zinc status is common in many low- and middle-income countries and is associated with stunting and infection.

Safety, upper limits and interactions

  • Long-term intake above the upper limit can cause copper deficiency, anemia and neurological problems.
  • Zinc nasal sprays have been linked to permanent loss of smell.
  • Zinc reduces absorption of quinolone and tetracycline antibiotics and penicillamine.

Reference values (NIH, adults)

ReferenceValue
Men (RDA)11 mg/day
Women (RDA)8 mg/day
Tolerable upper limit, adults40 mg/day

Regulatory context

  • United States: dietary supplements do not need FDA approval for safety or effectiveness before sale; manufacturers are responsible for safety and label claims.
  • Brazil: food supplements are regulated by Anvisa (RDC 243/2018 and its normative instructions), which define permitted ingredients, minimum and maximum amounts and allowed claims.

Limits of the evidence

Zinc status is hard to measure reliably; plasma zinc changes with inflammation and time of day. Trials use many different formulations.

This profile is educational. It does not recommend doses, products or brands. Talk to a health professional before starting supplements, especially if you take medicines, are pregnant or have a chronic condition.
