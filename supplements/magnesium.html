Magnesium

Magnesium is an essential mineral found in leafy greens, legumes, nuts, seeds and whole grains. About half of the body's magnesium is stored in bone.

Class
Essential mineral
Evidence summary
Deficiency correction: established. Blood pressure: small effect in meta-analyses. Sleep and cramps: weak or inconsistent.
Review status
Agent draft, 2026-10-31. Scientific review required.

What it does in the body

Magnesium is a cofactor for more than 300 enzyme systems, including those that produce energy (ATP), build protein and DNA, regulate blood glucose and control muscle and nerve function and blood pressure.

Who may be at risk of deficiency

  • Diets low in vegetables, legumes and whole grains; dietary surveys show many adults eat less than the RDA.
  • Gastrointestinal diseases such as Crohn's disease and celiac disease.
  • Type 2 diabetes and long-term alcohol use, which increase urinary losses.
  • Long-term use of proton-pump inhibitors or some diuretics.
  • Older adults.

Forms in supplements

Common forms include oxide, citrate, glycinate, chloride and lactate. Citrate, chloride and lactate are absorbed more completely than oxide in small studies; oxide and citrate have more laxative effect.

What human trials show

  • Meta-analysisA meta-analysis of 34 randomized trials found a small reduction in blood pressure (about 2/1.8 mmHg) with supplementation for about three months.
  • RandomizedTrials for nocturnal leg cramps in older adults have not shown a meaningful benefit (Cochrane review).
  • RandomizedSleep trials are few, small and short, with inconsistent results.

Observational evidence

  • Higher dietary magnesium intake is associated with lower risk of type 2 diabetes and stroke in cohort studies; these associations may reflect overall diet quality.

Safety, upper limits and interactions

  • Magnesium from food is not a concern; supplemental magnesium above the upper limit commonly causes diarrhea and cramping.
  • People with kidney disease are at risk of magnesium accumulation.
  • Magnesium can reduce absorption of bisphosphonates and tetracycline or quinolone antibiotics.

Reference values (NIH, adults)

ReferenceValue
Men 31+ (RDA)420 mg/day
Women 31+ (RDA)320 mg/day
Tolerable upper limit (supplements and medicines only)350 mg/day

Regulatory context

  • United States: dietary supplements do not need FDA approval for safety or effectiveness before sale; manufacturers are responsible for safety and label claims.
  • Brazil: food supplements are regulated by Anvisa (RDC 243/2018 and its normative instructions), which define permitted ingredients, minimum and maximum amounts and allowed claims.

Limits of the evidence

Many trials are small, use different forms and enroll people without measured deficiency. Blood magnesium is a poor marker of body stores.

This profile is educational. It does not recommend doses, products or brands. Talk to a health professional before starting supplements, especially if you take medicines, are pregnant or have a chronic condition.
