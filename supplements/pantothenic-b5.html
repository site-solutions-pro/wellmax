Pantothenic acid (vitamin B5)

Pantothenic acid (vitamin B5) is found in almost all foods, including meat, eggs, mushrooms, avocado and whole grains. Its name comes from the Greek for 'from everywhere'.

Class
Water-soluble vitamin (B5)
Evidence summary
Deficiency is very rare. Supplement claims (skin, energy, cholesterol): evidence unavailable or limited.
Review status
Agent draft, 2026-10-31. Scientific review required.

What it does in the body

Pantothenic acid is needed to make coenzyme A, which is central to the metabolism of fatty acids, carbohydrates and proteins, and to the synthesis of cholesterol and some hormones.

Who may be at risk of deficiency

  • Deficiency is very rare and has been seen mainly in severe malnutrition or in experiments.
  • Symptoms reported in such settings include numbness and burning of the feet, fatigue and irritability.

Forms in supplements

Supplements contain calcium pantothenate or panthenol. Pantethine, a related compound, has been studied for blood lipids. Dexpanthenol is used in creams for skin and wound care.

What human trials show

  • RandomizedSmall trials of pantethine reported modest reductions in LDL cholesterol; no trials of cardiovascular outcomes exist.
  • RandomizedOne small trial studied a pantothenic acid supplement for mild acne; evidence is too limited to draw conclusions.
  • Evidence unavailableThere is no reliable human evidence that pantothenic acid supplements improve energy or hair growth in people without deficiency.

Observational evidence

  • EVIDENCE_UNAVAILABLE: no consistent observational findings linking intake to disease in well-nourished populations.

Safety, upper limits and interactions

  • No tolerable upper limit has been set; very high intakes may cause mild diarrhea.
  • No well-documented medicine interactions.

Reference values (NIH, adults)

ReferenceValue
Adults (Adequate Intake)5 mg/day
Tolerable upper limitNot established (N/A)

Regulatory context

  • United States: dietary supplements do not need FDA approval for safety or effectiveness before sale; manufacturers are responsible for safety and label claims.
  • Brazil: food supplements are regulated by Anvisa (RDC 243/2018 and its normative instructions), which define permitted ingredients, minimum and maximum amounts and allowed claims.

Limits of the evidence

Pantothenic acid research is sparse. Results for pantethine should not be generalized to pantothenic acid.

This profile is educational. It does not recommend doses, products or brands. Talk to a health professional before starting supplements, especially if you take medicines, are pregnant or have a chronic condition.
