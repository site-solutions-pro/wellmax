Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA)

Omega-3 fatty acids include ALA from plants and EPA and DHA, found mainly in oily fish and algae. EPA and DHA are the forms studied in most heart and brain research.

Class
Long-chain fatty acids (EPA, DHA)
Evidence summary
Prescription EPA in high-risk patients: positive trial. General fish-oil supplements for heart prevention: mostly neutral.
Review status
Agent draft, 2026-10-31. Scientific review required.

What it does in the body

EPA and DHA are built into cell membranes, especially in the brain and retina, and are precursors of signaling molecules involved in inflammation. At high intakes they lower blood triglycerides.

Who may be at risk of deficiency

  • Clinical deficiency is rare in adults.
  • Intake of EPA and DHA is low in people who eat little or no fish.
  • The body converts only a small fraction of plant ALA to EPA and very little to DHA.

Forms in supplements

Products include fish oil, krill oil, algal oil and prescription formulations. Prescription products such as icosapent ethyl (purified EPA) differ from general supplements in purity and in the trials behind them.

What human trials show

  • RandomizedVITAL (25,871 adults): 1 g/day of marine omega-3 did not reduce major cardiovascular events or cancer in the general population.
  • RandomizedREDUCE-IT (8,179 statin-treated patients with high triglycerides and cardiovascular disease or diabetes): high-dose icosapent ethyl reduced major cardiovascular events by about 25% compared with a mineral-oil placebo.
  • RandomizedSTRENGTH, using a high-dose EPA plus DHA product with a corn-oil comparator, stopped early without benefit; the choice of placebo is debated as one explanation for the different results.

Observational evidence

  • Regular fish consumption is associated with lower cardiovascular mortality in cohort studies, which may reflect the whole diet rather than omega-3 alone.

Safety, upper limits and interactions

  • Meta-analyses of large trials show a higher risk of atrial fibrillation with omega-3 supplements, particularly at higher intakes.
  • May add to bleeding effects of anticoagulant and antiplatelet medicines.
  • Common effects include fishy aftertaste and digestive discomfort.

Regulatory context

  • United States: dietary supplements do not need FDA approval for safety or effectiveness before sale; manufacturers are responsible for safety and label claims.
  • Brazil: food supplements are regulated by Anvisa (RDC 243/2018 and its normative instructions), which define permitted ingredients, minimum and maximum amounts and allowed claims.

Limits of the evidence

Trial results depend strongly on the product, intake level, comparator oil and population. Results from purified prescription EPA should not be generalized to over-the-counter fish oil.

This profile is educational. It does not recommend doses, products or brands. Talk to a health professional before starting supplements, especially if you take medicines, are pregnant or have a chronic condition.
