Thiamin (vitamin B1)

Thiamin (vitamin B1) is found in whole grains, legumes, pork, fish and fortified cereals. The body stores little thiamin, so deficiency can develop within weeks of low intake.

Class
Water-soluble vitamin (B1)
Evidence summary
Deficiency treatment (beriberi, Wernicke encephalopathy): standard of care. Other uses: limited evidence.
Review status
Agent draft, 2026-10-31. Scientific review required.

What it does in the body

Thiamin pyrophosphate is a coenzyme in glucose metabolism and in the production of energy in nerve and heart cells.

Who may be at risk of deficiency

  • Alcohol use disorder, the most common cause in high-income countries.
  • After bariatric surgery, especially with prolonged vomiting.
  • People with heart failure on long-term loop diuretics.
  • Older adults and people with diabetes or HIV.
  • Severe deficiency causes beriberi (heart and nerve damage) and Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome (confusion, eye-movement problems and memory loss).

Forms in supplements

Supplements contain thiamin hydrochloride or mononitrate; benfotiamine is a fat-soluble derivative studied in diabetic neuropathy.

What human trials show

  • Standard of careThiamin is given urgently when Wernicke encephalopathy is suspected; this practice is based on clinical experience rather than large randomized trials.
  • RandomizedSmall trials of thiamin in heart failure have shown inconsistent effects on heart function.
  • RandomizedBenfotiamine trials in diabetic neuropathy are small with mixed results.

Observational evidence

  • Thiamin deficiency is common among hospitalized people with alcohol use disorder and is often missed.

Safety, upper limits and interactions

  • No tolerable upper limit has been set because no adverse effects have been reported from food or supplements.
  • Loop diuretics such as furosemide increase urinary thiamin losses.

Reference values (NIH, adults)

ReferenceValue
Men (RDA)1.2 mg/day
Women (RDA)1.1 mg/day
Tolerable upper limitNot established (N/A)

Regulatory context

  • United States: dietary supplements do not need FDA approval for safety or effectiveness before sale; manufacturers are responsible for safety and label claims.
  • Brazil: food supplements are regulated by Anvisa (RDC 243/2018 and its normative instructions), which define permitted ingredients, minimum and maximum amounts and allowed claims.

Limits of the evidence

Most evidence for thiamin outside deficiency comes from small studies. There is no reliable evidence that extra thiamin boosts energy in people who are not deficient.

This profile is educational. It does not recommend doses, products or brands. Talk to a health professional before starting supplements, especially if you take medicines, are pregnant or have a chronic condition.
